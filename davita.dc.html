Physician Order Entry

Physicians at DaVita could only sign orders in their own app. Every change went out loud to a nurse, who re-entered it in another system. I led the design that let physicians change orders themselves.

01The Problem

Every order change took two people and two systems

Care teams entered orders in CWOW. Physicians could only review and sign them in OneView, so every change went through a nurse.

02Discovery

Seeing the workflow where it happens

I joined as a new hire with no background on the problem, so the PM and I went to the clinics.

  • Shadowing on rounds

    We shadowed physicians in clinics across the country.

  • The relay, live

    We watched the spoken change, the nurse at the computer and the orders waiting to be signed.

  • What meetings missed

    The visits showed how little visibility and control physicians had inside OneView.

Setting the direction

With the PM, I wrote the PRD and the scope. Product chose a phased plan: prove value with a fast first release, earn buy-in and funding, then expand.

The phased plan

Prove value with a fast first release, earn buy-in and funding, then expand.

  1. Phase 1Treatment ordersRepurpose OneView’s Notes section to ship fast
  2. Phase 2Medication ordersBuild inside the existing prescription window
  3. LaterOne workflow; lab, diet and protocol ordersCut from scope

03Research

Where physicians want to change an order

I prototyped six places in OneView where an order change could start. Our UX researcher ran the study; I co-wrote the questions and built the prototypes.

6places in OneView prototyped as entry points
10physicians in the exploratory study
What we testedWhere order changes should live, how much patient data physicians need to change an order with confidence, when changes take effect, and which places to build first.

  • Outside the note

    Physicians want to change orders outside the documentation workflow, from one button rather than by picking categories.

  • Two moments

    They change orders during rounds, in the note, and while reviewing labs, in the patient chart. Those two entry points ranked highest.

  • A pop-up works

    Most were comfortable making the change in a pop-up.

  • Context depends on place

    The clinical summary needs more surrounding data than the other entry points.

  • History and a check

    They want to see data over time and confirm an order before it’s submitted.

Clinical buy-in

We presented the findings to DaVita’s Physician Advisory Board to test priorities and win support for moving order changes into OneView.

04Design

Ship fast inside what already exists

To validate early, leadership asked me to build Phase 1 inside OneView’s Notes section instead of a new interface.

Phase 1

Physicians could review, change and sign active treatment orders, in the clinic or for home dialysis, with no nurse in between. The first fields were treatment time and target weight.

Design decisions

Reuse the Notes section

Physicians are already there during rounds, and it cut build time.

Components built to grow

Fields and actions were added one at a time as leadership approved them, so nothing needed a redesign.

Work within OneView’s components

The component library was limited, so reusing what existed kept Phase 1 quick to build.

A confirm step

Physicians asked to double-check an order before submitting it.

The Phase 1 design sheet

The main workflow plus banners and alerts, tooltips, disabled states and state management.

The component sheet

Order panel components, built so new fields and actions could be added without a redesign.

What got in the way

  • Data access

    CWOW API failures, unclear data ownership and missing documentation meant dozens of Phase 1 variations as we worked around what was technically possible.

  • Misaligned leadership

    OneView and CWOW leaders disagreed on direction, which slowed decisions.

  • Late changes

    CWOW stakeholders pushed late requests that would have undone validated designs. I held the line where physician usability was at stake and compromised where it wasn’t.

More clinic shadowing and a design team off-site sharpened the final version.

05Release and Impact

06Phase 2

Medication orders: designed, then stalled

Medications raised the bar: more clinical risk, harder integration and less clear data ownership across systems.

  • Discovery

    The PM and I ran discovery sessions with developers and CWOW stakeholders to set the scope.

  • Familiar and cheap to build

    I designed medication ordering inside the existing prescription window. Physicians could add, change, discontinue and submit medication orders.

  • Edge cases and safety

    I iterated with clinical stakeholders on edge cases and safety, balancing speed against accuracy and compliance.

Where it landed

The design was complete and prototyped, but OneView and CWOW leadership never agreed on the vision or priorities, and months of delays followed. I chose to leave during Phase 2 and handed off every design, document and research finding so the next designer and my PM could carry it forward.

07What I Learned

The workflow was the product

Field time pays off

Shadowing physicians caught gaps no internal meeting would have surfaced.

Alignment is a design problem

The best-researched design stalls if teams don’t agree on vision, ownership and priorities, so I push to settle those early.

Design for what’s coming

Components built to grow saved rework every time the scope expanded.

Advocacy needs judgment

Holding the line on usability mattered, and so did knowing where to compromise to keep things moving.
